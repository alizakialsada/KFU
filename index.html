Introduction & Chemistry of Nucleic Acids

Molecular Biology

Study of DNA, RNA, proteins and interactions; links molecular biology to drug action and pharmacogenomics.

DNA vs RNA

DNA: deoxyribose, thymine, usually double-stranded. RNA: ribose, uracil, usually single-stranded.

Nitrogenous bases

Purines: adenine and guanine. Pyrimidines: cytosine, thymine and uracil.

Nucleotide vs Nucleoside

Nucleoside = base + sugar. Nucleotide = base + sugar + phosphate(s).

Phosphodiester bond

Nucleotides join via 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkages to form a sugar–phosphate backbone.

Complementarity

A pairs T with 2 hydrogen bonds; G pairs C with 3. Strands are antiparallel.

Chargaff’s rule

In double-stranded DNA, %A=%T and %G=%C; total purines equals total pyrimidines.

Sequence questions

For a 5′→3′ strand, write its antiparallel complement and reverse when reporting the complementary strand 5′→3′.

Nucleic acid roles

mRNA carries information; tRNA is an adaptor; rRNA is ribosomal. ATP is an energy carrier; NAD is a cofactor.

Chromosomes and history

Human somatic cells typically have 46 chromosomes; gametes 23. Watson–Crick model: 1953; Avery et al.: 1944.

Nucleotide anatomy

The nitrogenous base is attached to sugar; a phosphate group creates a nucleotide. Purines are bicyclic (A, G) while pyrimidines are single-ring (C, T, U).

DNA base-percentage calculation

For double-stranded DNA, A=T and G=C. Example: A=20%, T=20%, G=30%, C=30%; the single-strand rule cannot automatically be assumed.

Chemical linkage and orientation

A 3′-OH of one nucleotide connects to the 5′ phosphate of the next. Always report sequence orientation explicitly, normally 5′→3′.

Major milestones

Avery–MacLeod–McCarty associated DNA with genetic material (1944); Watson–Crick double helix (1953); Human Genome Project completed (2003).

W3–4

Organization of DNA and RNA

DNA denaturation

Heat, high pH, organic solvents, and low salt conditions can separate complementary DNA strands. Denaturation disrupts noncovalent interactions (hydrogen bonds and base stacking) but does NOT break the covalent 3′–5′ phosphodiester backbone.

Melting temperature (Tm)

Tm is the temperature at which 50% of the DNA helical structure is denatured. At temperatures above Tm more DNA molecules exist as separated single strands. Tm depends on sequence and solution conditions.

GC content and DNA stability

GC-rich DNA generally has a higher Tm than AT-rich DNA of similar length under the same conditions. GC pairs have three hydrogen bonds (AT: two), and sequence-dependent base stacking also contributes.

Hyperchromic effect at 260 nm

DNA denaturation increases absorbance at 260 nm (A260), called the hyperchromic effect, as bases become less stacked and more exposed.

Renaturation / annealing

Separated complementary DNA strands can reform a duplex under appropriate conditions. The lecture gives an optimal temperature approximately 25°C below Tm; successful annealing also depends on DNA concentration and incubation time.

Factors affecting renaturation

Temperature: sufficiently below Tm for stable pairing. Concentration: higher complementary strand concentration increases encounter probability (within limits). Time: longer incubation allows further renaturation.

Nucleic-acid hybridization

Hybridization is complementary base pairing between strands to form DNA–DNA, DNA–RNA, or RNA–RNA duplexes; sequences may be fully or nearly complementary.

Hybridization applications

PCR depends on primer annealing; Southern blot detects DNA, Northern blot detects RNA, FISH visualizes chromosomes or loci, and microarrays assay hybridization to many probes.

Why DNA packaging matters

A human cell contains approximately 2 m of DNA, so chromosomal DNA must be compacted yet remain accessible to enzymes and regulated genes.

Histones: charge and composition

Histones are positively charged at physiological pH because they are rich in basic amino acids lysine and arginine. Negatively charged DNA phosphates interact electrostatically with histones.

Core histones and the octamer

A nucleosome core contains two copies EACH of H2A, H2B, H3, and H4: a total of 8 core histone proteins (histone octamer).

Histone H1

H1 is the linker histone and is NOT part of the core histone octamer. It binds near DNA entry/exit and linker DNA and favors higher-order packing. The lecture describes H1 as the most tissue- and species-specific histone.

Nucleosome: DNA length and turns

About 147 base pairs wrap around the histone octamer in approximately 1.65–1.7 turns. Neighboring nucleosomes are separated by linker DNA (~50 bp in the lecture).

Chromatin and chromosomes

Chromatin = DNA plus its associated proteins; chromosomes are discrete chromatin structures. Nucleosomes are the fundamental repeating units of eukaryotic chromatin.

Hierarchy of DNA packaging

DNA double helix → nucleosomes (beads on a string) → polynucleosome / nucleofilament → compact chromatin fibers and looped domains → chromosome. The lecture includes the traditional 30-nm fiber model.

Histone-tail modifications

Histone N-terminal tails may be acetylated, methylated, or phosphorylated. These reversible modifications influence DNA accessibility and gene expression; acetylation is generally associated with more open transcriptionally active chromatin.

Euchromatin versus heterochromatin

Euchromatin is relatively open and generally transcriptionally active. Heterochromatin is more tightly packed and generally less transcriptionally active; do not reverse the two.

Centromere

The centromere is a specialized chromosome domain essential for kinetochore assembly and accurate chromosome segregation during cell division.

Telomere

Telomeres consist of repeated DNA sequences at chromosome ends and help protect chromosome-end stability; they are different from centromeres.

RNA chemical composition

RNA is a polymer of ribonucleotides containing ribose with a 2′-OH, the bases A/G/C/U, a phosphodiester backbone, and 5′→3′ directionality.

RNA secondary structure

Although RNA is commonly single-stranded, it forms intramolecular base pairs and stems, loops, bulges, hairpins, and internal loops. RNA pairing includes A–U and G–C.

RNA tertiary structure

RNA can fold into compact globular shapes. Hydrogen bonding, base stacking, and the ribose 2′-OH contribute to structures essential for tRNA, rRNA, and catalytic ribozymes.

RNA hairpin and inverted repeats

Inverted-repeat complementary regions within one RNA strand can pair to create a hairpin/stem-loop. A loop is usually largely unpaired.

Palindromic sequences

A DNA palindrome has reverse-complement symmetry when strands are read 5′→3′, not merely the same letters read left-to-right. Inverted repeats permit hairpin/cruciform formation.

Mirror repeats versus inverted repeats

Inverted repeat symmetry is based on complementary reverse sequences; mirror repeat is symmetry within a single strand, which is not equivalent to a DNA palindrome.

rRNA and ribozyme activity

rRNA supplies the ribosome’s structural and catalytic core. Peptidyl transferase activity is RNA-based: the ribosome is a ribozyme.

Bacterial 70S ribosome

70S = 30S (16S rRNA) + 50S (23S + 5S rRNAs). Svedberg values measure sedimentation and do not add arithmetically.

Eukaryotic 80S ribosome

80S = 40S (18S rRNA) + 60S (28S, 5.8S, 5S rRNAs); the large subunit contains peptidyl-transferase center.

mRNA

Messenger RNA carries coding information from DNA to the ribosome where it serves as the translation template.

tRNA

Transfer RNA is the adaptor that recognizes mRNA codons through its anticodon and delivers corresponding amino acids; mature tRNA has a 3′-CCA attachment end.

miRNA / siRNA

miRNAs and siRNAs direct sequence-specific gene silencing through Argonaute-containing complexes.

snRNA

Small nuclear RNAs are components of the spliceosome, involved in pre-mRNA splicing.

snoRNA

Small nucleolar RNAs help direct chemical modification and processing of rRNA in the nucleolus.

SRP RNA (7SL)

The RNA component of the signal-recognition particle helps direct nascent secretory or membrane proteins to the appropriate membrane targeting pathway.

lncRNA

Long noncoding RNA can serve as scaffolds, guides, decoys, or regulators of chromatin and transcription.

Essential compare-and-contrast

DNA denaturation vs phosphodiester cleavage; annealing vs hybridization; H1 vs H2A/H2B/H3/H4; centromere vs telomere; 70S vs 80S; snRNA vs snoRNA; inverted vs mirror repeats.

W5–6

DNA Replication

Semiconservative replication

Each daughter DNA duplex has one parental and one newly synthesized strand.

Replication origins and forks

Replication initiates at origins; a bidirectional active origin produces two forks.

Helicase

ATP-dependent helicase unwinds the parental duplex at replication forks.

Single-strand binding proteins

SSB proteins stabilize single-stranded DNA and prevent reannealing.

Topoisomerase / gyrase

Topoisomerases relieve torsional stress; Type II can cut both DNA strands transiently.

Primase / primer

Primase can initiate an RNA primer de novo; DNA polymerases need a pre-existing 3′-OH.

Polymerization direction

DNA polymerase synthesizes 5′→3′ while reading template 3′→5′.

Leading and lagging strands

Leading strand grows continuously toward the fork; lagging strand grows discontinuously as Okazaki fragments.

Okazaki fragment maturation

In bacteria DNA polymerase I removes/replaces RNA primers and ligase seals nicks.

Proofreading

Many replicative polymerases remove wrongly incorporated bases using 3′→5′ exonuclease activity.

Telomeres and telomerase

Telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein reverse transcriptase; its RNA templates TTAGGG repeat addition in humans.

DNA polymerase I versus III (bacteria)

DNA polymerase III is the major replicative polymerase. DNA polymerase I removes RNA primers and replaces them with DNA; DNA ligase seals remaining nicks.

Processivity and strand coordination

A replisome coordinates continuous leading-strand and looping lagging-strand synthesis; both new strands grow 5′→3′.

Topoisomerase II mechanism

Type II topoisomerases transiently break both DNA strands, pass another DNA duplex segment through and reseal breaks to resolve torsional stress.

Fidelity and mismatch repair

3′→5′ proofreading exonuclease removes incorrectly added bases during replication; mismatch repair can correct errors that escape proofreading.

Telomerase mechanism

TERT is reverse transcriptase protein; TERC is intrinsic RNA template. Human telomeric repeats are TTAGGG, and extension of 3′ overhang precedes complementary-strand fill-in.

W7–8

Transcription

Replication vs transcription

Transcription selectively makes RNA from one DNA template strand, without primer; genome replication makes DNA and needs primer.

Template / coding strands

RNA is synthesized 5′→3′; the coding DNA has matching sequence except T is U in RNA.

RNA types

mRNA, tRNA, rRNA and regulatory RNAs serve different cellular roles.

Bacterial RNA polymerase

Core α₂ββ′ω; holoenzyme includes σ factor for promoter recognition.

Bacterial promoter

σ70 −35 region TTGACA; −10 Pribnow box TATAAT; transcription starts at +1.

Initiation and elongation

Closed complex → open complex → promoter escape → elongation.

Intrinsic termination

GC-rich RNA hairpin followed by U-rich tract destabilizes transcription complex.

Rho-dependent termination

Rho is an ATP-dependent RNA translocase, binds rut site and travels 5′→3′.

Eukaryotic RNA polymerases

Pol I: 18S, 5.8S, 28S rRNA; Pol II: mRNA; Pol III: tRNA and 5S rRNA.

Regulatory elements

Promoters/enhancers are cis-acting DNA regions; TFs are generally trans-acting.

General transcription factors

TFIID/TBP recognizes promoter; TFIIH unwinds DNA and phosphorylates RNA Pol II CTD.

mRNA 5′ cap

7-methylguanosine connected by 5′–5′ triphosphate bond protects RNA and helps translation.

Polyadenylation

AAUAAA signal, cleavage and poly(A) addition support stability and expression.

Splicing

Spliceosome removes introns, joins exons; branch-point A and lariat intermediate.

tRNA and rRNA maturation

RNase P trims tRNA 5′ end; CCA at 3′; 45S precursor yields 18S, 5.8S, 28S rRNAs.

Bacteria vs eukaryotes

Bacteria often couple transcription and translation; eukaryotes separate them and process pre-mRNA extensively.

Promoter recognition and sigma

Bacterial core RNA polymerase α₂ββ′ω becomes holoenzyme with σ. Sigma binds −35 and −10 promoter elements; promoter escape follows short initial RNA synthesis.

RNA polymerase transcription bubble

The RNA polymerase reads template DNA 3′→5′ and synthesizes RNA 5′→3′ without a primer; local DNA is unwound and re-annealed.

Rho-dependent termination details

Rho is an ATP-driven hexameric helicase/translocase; it binds rut on nascent RNA, moves 5′→3′ and catches paused RNA polymerase.

Eukaryotic initiation factors

TFIID includes TBP; TFIIH opens DNA and phosphorylates Pol II CTD. Cis DNA enhancers interact with trans transcription factors, mediator and chromatin remodelers.

Pre-mRNA processing landmarks

5′ cap is 7-methylguanosine via 5′–5′ triphosphate; poly(A) cleavage signal often AAUAAA; spliceosome uses 5′ splice site, branch point A, 3′ splice site and lariat.

Three RNA polymerases and rRNA

Pol I produces 45S precursor for 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs; Pol II produces mRNA; Pol III produces tRNA and 5S rRNA.

W3–4

Organization of DNA and RNA

Define Tm and describe the effect of increased GC content.

Tm is temperature at which 50% of DNA helices are denatured; more GC generally raises Tm under comparable conditions.

Why does A260 increase during DNA denaturation?

The hyperchromic effect arises because disruption of base stacking exposes bases and increases UV absorbance.

Name three factors that influence DNA renaturation.

Temperature (lecture ~25°C below Tm), DNA concentration, and renaturation time.

What are DNA–RNA hybrids and which methods use hybridization?

Complementary DNA and RNA strands can base-pair; examples include PCR primer annealing, Southern/Northern blotting, FISH, and microarrays.

Name all histones of the core octamer and distinguish H1.

Two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4; H1 is linker histone, not a core histone.

How many base pairs wrap around one histone octamer?

Approximately 147 bp, ~1.65–1.7 turns; linker DNA is ~50 bp in these slides.

What is the difference between centromeres and telomeres?

Centromeres are involved in chromosome segregation; telomeres are repetitive protective chromosome ends.

List the structural features of an RNA hairpin.

Intramolecular complementary stem with base-paired regions and an unpaired loop; bulges and internal loops may occur.

What is a palindromic DNA sequence?

A sequence with reverse-complement symmetry when each strand is written 5′→3′.

Compare bacterial and eukaryotic ribosomal rRNAs.

Bacteria: 30S/16S and 50S/23S+5S; eukaryotes: 40S/18S and 60S/28S+5.8S+5S.

What makes the ribosome a ribozyme?

Its peptidyl-transferase catalytic center is RNA-based.

Give the functions of snRNA, snoRNA, miRNA/siRNA, SRP RNA, and lncRNA.

snRNA splicing; snoRNA rRNA modification; miRNA/siRNA silencing; SRP RNA protein targeting; lncRNA scaffolds/guides/regulators.

True or false: Histones are rich in acidic amino acids.

False. Histones are enriched in basic lysine and arginine.

True or false: Some RNA molecules behave as enzymes.

True. Ribozymes include rRNA catalytic activity.

True or false: Nucleosomes are the basis of higher-order chromatin packaging.

True. Nucleosomes are the basic repeating unit of chromatin.

True or false: At temperatures above Tm, more DNA is denatured than at Tm.

True. Tm is the half-denatured point; a higher temperature generally further shifts DNA toward single strands.

True or false: Euchromatin is tightly packed and genetically inactive.

False. Heterochromatin is generally tighter; euchromatin is relatively open and transcriptionally active.

W5–6

DNA Replication

Why are helicase and topoisomerase both required at a replication fork?

Helicase opens the helix; topoisomerase removes torsional stress ahead of the fork.

What is the difference between a template strand and a primer?

Template specifies which bases to incorporate; primer supplies a free 3′-OH for extension.

Why can primase start synthesis de novo but DNA polymerase cannot?

Primase initiates without a 3′-OH; DNA polymerase requires a primer 3′-OH.

How many replication forks are created by one active origin?

Two, for bidirectional replication.

Why are SSB proteins essential after helicase opens the duplex?

They prevent complementary strands from reannealing and protect single-stranded DNA.

How do leading- and lagging-strand synthesis differ?

Leading is continuous; lagging is discontinuous via Okazaki fragments.

What are Okazaki fragments, and how are they converted into a continuous strand?

Short lagging-strand DNA pieces; RNA primers are removed and replaced with DNA, then ligase joins the pieces.

W7–8

Transcription

What is a promoter, and why is it not transcribed itself?

A DNA element recognized to position RNA polymerase; upstream recognition elements are usually outside the transcribed region.

What subunit or factor helps bacterial RNA polymerase recognize promoters?

The σ (sigma) factor.

How do intrinsic and Rho-dependent termination differ mechanistically?

Intrinsic: GC hairpin plus U tract; Rho-dependent: ATP-driven Rho binds rut and catches paused RNAP.

Why is transcription generally less accurate than DNA replication?

Transcription generally has less stringent fidelity mechanisms than genome replication.

Why can transcription and translation be coupled in bacteria but not in eukaryotes?

Both occur in same bacterial compartment, while eukaryotic transcription occurs in the nucleus and translation in the cytoplasm.

🟡 Study Notes · Key Topics

Organization

Histone octamer

H2A, H2B, H3 and H4: two copies of each (eight core histones). Basic, positively charged histones bind negatively charged DNA; DNA charge comes from phosphate groups.

Organization

Nucleosome and H1

~147 bp of DNA wrap around the histone octamer (~1.65 turns). Histone H1 binds linker DNA and is not in the octamer.

Organization

Chromatin states

Heterochromatin is tightly packed and generally transcriptionally inactive; euchromatin is less condensed and generally gene-active.

Organization

Centromere / telomere

Centromere participates in chromosome segregation; telomeres distinguish and protect chromosome ends. Human telomeric repeat: TTAGGG (a repeated cyclic shift can look like AGGGTT).

Organization

RNA structure

RNA contains A, G, C, U and ribose with 2′-OH; commonly single-stranded but forms intramolecular secondary/tertiary structures.

Organization

Palindromic inverted repeats

Complementary inverted repeats in one RNA strand can base-pair to form a stem–loop (hairpin). The professor explicitly indicated a sequence-design/recognition question.

Organization

RNA types

Know mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, snRNA, miRNA and siRNA roles; lecturer indicated some less-common RNA subtypes were not to be memorized in detail.

Replication

Origin of replication (ori)

Specific DNA sequence where initiation proteins assemble. Replication proceeds bidirectionally from origins; eukaryotes have multiple origins and typical bacterial chromosomes usually one.

Replication

Four key principles

Bidirectional, semiconservative, 5′→3′ synthesis, and semidiscontinuous (leading continuous / lagging discontinuous).

Replication

Semiconservative mechanism

Each daughter DNA duplex receives one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand.

Replication

3′-OH requirement

DNA polymerase extends an existing strand by adding nucleotides to the free 3′-OH; new DNA always grows 5′→3′.

Replication

dNTP vs NTP

DNA polymerization uses dATP, dTTP, dGTP and dCTP; RNA synthesis uses ribonucleoside triphosphates (NTPs). The transcripts have occasional speech-to-text slips in base names.

Replication

Leading and lagging

Leading strand is continuous; lagging strand is built as Okazaki fragments, requiring multiple RNA primers.

Replication

Polymerase III vs I (bacteria)

Pol III is principal replicative enzyme. Pol I removes RNA primers through 5′→3′ exonuclease activity and fills DNA gaps; ligase seals remaining nicks.

Replication

Proofreading

Many DNA polymerases remove mismatched bases using 3′→5′ exonuclease activity; do not confuse with 5′→3′ DNA synthesis.

Replication

Cell cycle

DNA is duplicated during the S (synthesis) phase. The professor explicitly asked which phase contains DNA replication.

Replication

Eukaryotic polymerases

Lecturer highlighted Pol ε (leading), Pol δ (lagging), and Pol γ (mitochondrial DNA); Pol α participates in primer initiation. This is simplified lecture framing.

Replication

Telomere end-replication problem

Removal of the terminal lagging-strand RNA primer leaves a gap lacking an upstream 3′-OH; telomerase uses an intrinsic RNA template to extend telomere DNA.

Replication / drugs

Topoisomerase drug targets

Lecturer contrasted camptothecin-type Topo I agents, doxorubicin acting on Topo II, and fluoroquinolones acting on bacterial DNA gyrase/topoisomerase IV.

Replication / drugs

Nucleoside analogs

Drugs that mimic natural nucleosides and are converted to active phosphorylated forms can compete during DNA synthesis and block chain extension.

DNA Repair

Base excision repair (BER)

Small non-bulky base lesions: DNA glycosylase → AP endonuclease → DNA polymerase → ligase. Lecturer emphasized the enzyme sequence.

DNA Repair

Nucleotide excision repair (NER)

Bulky helix-distorting lesions, including UV-induced pyrimidine dimers; damaged oligonucleotide excised, followed by polymerase and ligase.

DNA Repair

Xeroderma pigmentosum

Lecturer connected defective NER with sensitivity to UV damage and skin cancer risk.

DNA Repair

Mismatch repair (MMR)

Corrects replication-associated mismatches that escape polymerase proofreading; professor said detailed names of some participating proteins are not necessary.

Transcription

RNA polymerase

Transcription synthesizes RNA from DNA without an RNA primer; uses NTPs and synthesizes RNA 5′→3′.

Transcription

Bacterial sigma factor

RNA polymerase holoenzyme includes sigma, which guides promoter recognition; lecturer discusses locating the promoter before initiation.
